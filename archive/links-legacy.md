## 2026-08-24

[Fences, Not Sandboxes](https://yegge.ai/essays/fences-not-sandboxes/) is one of the best descriptions of what it is like to work with AI agents, long term, on complex projects.

Agents work best in verifiable domains like software code, but when they have to work in hard-to-verify domains agents will invent rules that target some kind of measurable success metric. As you work on a complex project with an AI agent you would do well to keep this in mind, if only to illuminate why they do some of the things they do.

In the end, this is not that different than working with people. Anyone who's been a manager knows that there are *the rules you outline* and then there are *the rules that grow and appear* between people and in people's minds. These rules are invented because they help people get things done, but of course the danger is that they can, if unchecked, degrade or run counter to explicit objectives.

## 2026-08-09b

Jasmine Sun wrote a pretty good [report on opposition to data centers](https://jasmi.news/p/no-data-centers-in-my-backyard), but this passage caught my attention:

> It turns out that politics is more than an optimization game; voter interests are neither stable nor reducible to dollar amounts. It is also about *thymos*: the desire to be “recognized as a human being… with a certain worth or dignity,” who can sacrifice material survival in service of higher ideals. Through Fukuyama’s lens, elections and town halls and social media debates matter not because they are maximally efficient preference aggregators, but because they are “a stage for the expression of thymos”—a place where people make their values heard.

This is an insight that is both true and ancient, one a Roman Senator might have passed down to a junior staff member two and a half thousand years ago.

I've [said before](#2026-04-25) that the spread of the engineering mindset might wind up being one of the largest second-order consequences of AI adoption, and I think this is a perfect example. To an engineer everything is an "optimization game" where actors are trying to get the best result for themselves, in an Adam Smith *Invisible Hand* kind of way. But if the engineering mindset is all you know the world will remain mystifying and you'll need to rediscover ancient insights from first principals.

## 2025-02-17

The Economist has a pretty good write-up on [the danger of relying on OpenAI's Deep Research](https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2025/02/13/the-danger-of-relying-on-openais-deep-research), which basically matches my experience. It misses, however, how tools like this can help you overcome little barriers in life.

For example, this morning I used it for 2 things and it saved me tons of time:

1. I wanted to find a company that comes and picks up stuff you want to get rid of and either recycles, donates, or sells it. And wanted to avoid a place that scoops up valuables and takes the rest to the dump. In about 10 minutes it found [remoovit.com](http://remoovit.com), which is pretty much exactly what I want.
2. I was not directly threatened by the [Eaton fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eaton_Fire) but we are nearby and left because the air was filled with ash and because 🤷! But we soon realized we didn't leave the house with everything we should have. We took passports but forgot birth certificates, etc. So I asked Deep Resarch to create a checklist for a "grab and go disaster box" with all life's critical paperwork and valuables. Again, in about 10 minutes I had a really good starting point which I printed out and will start building. Now, would it have been faster to google around and find an existing checklist? Maybe. But this was tailored to my exact needs and requests and I spent those 10 minutes making coffee and talking to my kids instead of googling.

These tools make easy the things you're currently putting off because there's a "toil barrier" you don't have the energy or resources to overcome. Those things can seem trivially small but small gains add up over time.

## 2024-12-21

[*The Unbearable Slowness of Being*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10234) is a paper that explores the large discrepancy between the brain's sensory input capacity (around a billion bits per second) and the brain's much slower processing capacity (about 10 bits per second).

This is fascinating to me because it aligns with the ideas in Daniel Kahneman's brilliant book [*Thinking, Fast and Slow*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow), which explores the same ideas from a more qualitative perspective.

It also strikes me that this validates much of what Bret Victor (et al.) is exploring with [Dynamicland](https://dynamicland.org). If the brain works as this paper suggests, moving computation from the narrow constraints of a screen designed for serialized processing to an environment that taps into our richer, parallel sensory channels isn't just interesting -- it's a fundamentally better paradigm because it aligns with the way our brains are built to process information.

## 2023-07-12b

From [McKinsey's Quarterly Interview with Jony Ive](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/mckinsey-digital/our-insights/the-creative-process-is-fabulously-unpredictable-a-great-idea-cannot-be-predicted#/):

>

>
> When you gather a large group of people, they generally want to be able to relate to one another and to be sociable. But any process that is unpredictable does not sit comfortably or naturally in a large group setting. So people come to value activities that are predictable.
>

That's why we choose to talk about schedule or cost or speed or weight. Given our very different backgrounds, that's a comfortable and easy conversation. I completely get it.
>
> But there's an insidious problem with that. There is a dangerous assumption that we're having these conversations because they're the only important ones. But the really important conversations and preoccupations and concerns are very hard. Because you can't assign a system of numbers to make the relative judgments that need to be made.
>
> I used to think that this kind of conversation was a personal attack, or an affront to the practice of creating, but I've come to learn, over the years, that it's just a natural, very predictable consequence of having larger numbers of people gather together to talk about developing something.
